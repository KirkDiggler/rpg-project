How a backend architect made a game

The Invisible Half

I did not model him, rig him, or light that room. I built everything you cannot see. This is the story of how long that took, and what finally made it visible.

Kirk Gilmore · rpg-toolkit, rpg-api, rpg-dnd5e-web · built in the open

↓ the whole thing, properly

I had been out of video games for about five years when the itch came back. There was no plan attached to it. I just knew I had to build something.

What I want to tell you about is not really a game engine, even though that is what I ended up with. It is about finding out what kind of creative person you are, and then spending fourteen months proving it.

The beginning · one pull request

“This is what you wanted to add?”

The thing that started it was a Mastodon bot that ran adventures using polls. You would vote on what the party did next, and the story moved. I loved it. I contributed exactly one piece of code to it.

It was not a feature. I had found a piece of logic that was messy and I simplified it so it read clearly. The maintainer’s comment back to me was, more or less: this is what you wanted to add?

He was not being unkind. He was surprised. Out of everything that project could have used, the thing I reached for was making a tangled piece of code easy to understand.

I have thought about that comment ever since, and it is the honest starting point of this whole story — not because I decided to build a game that day. I did not, for a long time. But somebody had accidentally told me what kind of builder I am.

I am a creative. It is just architectural creativity, and not so much on the visual side.

What it unlocked Permission to build the part I am actually good at, and to stop treating it as a lesser kind of making because nobody can see it.
First shape · a Discord bot

Forms meant character creation

So I started building a D&D bot for Discord, thinking I would follow roughly what the Mastodon bot did. Polls to steer the adventure, text coming back.

Then I found out Discord bots could have forms. That changed what the project was, immediately — because a form meant character creation was possible. Not voting on what the party does next. Actually sitting down and making someone.

That was my first real piece of excitement on this. Somewhere around then I built an ASCII art scene to show a room, and I was thrilled with that too. Which is the honest scale of the thing at that point: a character sheet you could fill in, and a room drawn in text characters.

What it unlocked A character somebody owns. Everything after this had a person on the other end who had made something and cared what happened to it.
The turn · Activities and Three.js

It stops being a bot

Two things landed close together. Discord has Activities — you can run a real web application inside a voice channel, with everyone in it. And Three.js will put actual 3D in a browser.

That was the moment it got out of the little Discord bot project. The shape started to take hold. If the thing on the other end can be a room you look at instead of a paragraph you read, then the thing underneath it has to be a real rules engine, not a pile of bot commands.

I did not have that engine. I had a bot. So I went and built one, and that is where the next year went.

What it unlocked A ceiling high enough to be worth building a foundation for.
June 2025 · the engine begins

The question that was too small

I sat down to answer what I thought was a contained question: what is a condition? Being poisoned, being blessed, being enraged. How do I represent that?

I was asking at the wrong scope. A condition, a class feature, a weapon enchantment, a spell effect and difficult terrain are all the same shape of thing — something that hooks into what is happening and changes it. Once I saw that, the little bus I had built to pass combat modifiers around turned out to be the thing everything else plugs into.

From the record

The note from that week puts it more bluntly than I would have: “we realized our event bus isn’t just for conditions — it’s the nervous system of the entire game.”

The other half of that month was learning to take my own D&D back out of the engine. I built a concentration manager, realised it was too specific to one game’s rules, and tore it out. I put a method called ProcessShortRest() on a generic resource pool and flagged it in my own notes as a red flag. Both times the fix was the same: the engine should never learn what a short rest is. It only needs to know something happened, and ask each thing whether it cares.

What it unlocked One mechanism instead of five. Dawn restoring a paladin’s power, a story milestone refilling a resource, a spell topping up an ally — none of which are in the rulebook, none of which needed new engine code.
August 2025 · the week it cracked

Rage was supposed to be a hundred lines

Barbarian rage. You get angry, you hit harder, you take less damage. I estimated about a hundred lines.

In 5e, rage ends on its own if you go a round without attacking anyone or being hit. So rage listens for the end of your turn, checks, and if it is over it has to stop listening. That is where it stopped dead. The event system held a lock while it was handing the event out to listeners, and un-listening needed that same lock. The feature could not switch itself off. A barbarian could not stop being angry.

The fix is the first thing I built that I would still defend today: a listener never reaches into the machinery mid-flight. It returns what it wants to happen, and the machinery does it once the event is finished. Events complete, then the world changes.

That was the good part. The rest of that attempt was bad, and it was bad in ways that were entirely my own fault — untyped strings everywhere, and infrastructure I had already written that I ignored and then rebuilt worse. I threw the whole pull request away and filed five issues out of the wreckage.

From the record

Two lines I wrote that night and still stand by: “Sometimes the best code is the code you don’t ship.” And “Better to have no combat than broken combat.”

What came out over the next thirty-six hours is the thing I am proudest of in the whole engine. Rage became one self-contained object. It listens for three things, keeps track of whether you swung and whether you got hit, and when neither happened it takes itself off the board. Nothing polls it. No combat code anywhere asks “is this character raging?”

Want a new effect? Write the function and attach it to a player, a monster, a trap. It manages itself. Nobody keeps a master list.

What it unlocked Content stopped being engine work. A new condition became a small self-contained thing somebody writes, not a change to how combat works.
The same week · the quiet one

How does a rules engine save a game?

This is the one I genuinely did not know how to answer when I started, and it is the one that turned out to matter most.

If the server stores your character, and your character has a rage with two uses left and a bless that expires in four rounds — does the server have to understand what rage is? Because if it does, I have just spread the rules across two codebases and I will be chasing them forever.

The answer was to make every piece describe itself. A feature knows how to write itself down and how to read itself back. The server stores a blob it cannot interpret. When it comes back, the engine reads exactly one field — a name like dnd5e:feature:rage — and hands the whole thing to whoever owns that name.

The game server never learns what rage is.

From the record

It is one line, buried in a list near the end of a document from the least confident day of that whole week — a day where I wrote four documents and decided nothing in any of them: “ToJSON for storage, ToData for internals.”

Fourteen months later it is load-bearing across the entire codebase. The idea I was actually excited about that week is the one that did not ship.

What it unlocked A rules engine and a game server that can be built, changed and reasoned about separately — which is the only reason one person could keep both of them moving.
August 2025 · the first time

“I have a place to put everything”

There is a feeling that has arrived three times on this project, and I only see the pattern now that I am writing it down. This was the first, and it came the moment that bus existed:

I have a place to put all of the things I need.

I did not, quite. What I actually had — and I only learned this by using it — was a place to put conditions, and a place to put things that interrupt a chain of events. That is a lot. It is not everything. But it was the first time the shape of the project felt bigger than the list of features I had written down.

Autumn 2025 · the payoff you can see

A +2 shows up in a combat log

Most of this story happens before anybody can look at anything. This is the first moment where that is not true.

A barbarian rages. The raging condition attaches itself to the damage calculation. And the +2 appears, itemised, in the breakdown a player reads. Not a number that came from somewhere. A number with its reasons attached.

And then I went and did something else for four months.

What it unlocked Proof, for the first time, that any of the invisible work reached a human being.
January – April 2026

No design documents. No architecture decisions. Ten merged pull requests across three repositories in February.

The detour · and it was a good one

I got pulled into the thing I was building with

I know exactly what those gaps are, and it was not that I gave up on this.

My actual work got extremely interesting. Around that autumn I realised I could put an agent in our cloud and need nothing more than a browser to reach our admin site. And I had been using AI to help me build this game — which is when it turned over: I could be building the AI that helps, and that helps build other things too.

So I went into deep research and some fairly crazy implementations, and this project sat still while I did. That is the whole explanation. It was not a crisis of faith in the game. It was four months somewhere else that turned out to be worth going.

Coming back from it, I had to reorient myself.

What it unlocked The way I now work on this at all — and the reason one person can move a rules engine, a server and a 3D client at the same time.
May 2026 · coming back

An audit of my own project

Reorienting meant inventory, not inspiration. I came back to a large codebase I had written myself and re-derived it by searching through it, because I no longer trusted my memory of it.

Two of the packages sitting on my own combat hot path were not mentioned anywhere in my documentation. And something I would have told you confidently about my own architecture came back marked partially true — needs correction.

What it unlocked Honest ground to build on, and the habit of checking the code instead of quoting the plan.
July 2026 · the part I could not do

I found out I could just buy the halfling

Here is the thing I had been quietly stuck on for a year, and it was never the engine.

I cannot model a character. I cannot texture a crypt or animate a greatclub swing. That is a real wall for one person, and no amount of architecture gets you over it. For a long time the honest answer to “when will this look like a game?” was that it would not.

Then two things changed at once. I found Synty asset packs — professional low-poly art, buyable, in bulk. And the AI tooling got good enough that I could move at the pace my design thinking was already running at, across a rules engine and a server and a 3D client at the same time, on my own.

Development did not speed up a little. In the month after the art landed, more work shipped across the three repositories than in any month in the project’s history — against a trough of ten merged pull requests five months earlier.

Being fair about it

Two things changed in that window, not one. The art arrived and the way I was working changed. I am not going to claim the assets alone did that.

But the reason it worked is the fourteen months above it. When the halfling arrived, the engine did not have to change to accept him. He could walk, swing, open a door, and be seen by a monster that reasoned about him — because none of that had ever been about what he looked like.

What it unlocked A backend architect could suddenly ship a game that looks like a game. That is the whole reason this page exists.
The one piece of game design I am proud of

The dice are on the floor

Throwing dice is a huge part of what a tabletop game actually is. It is the bit everyone leans in for. So in this game we roll them the way you would at a table: on the dungeon floor, in front of everyone.

They are real objects. They bounce off walls. They can fall off the table. When a check happens, the die is there, on the ground where somebody left it, and you go and click it.

The piece I like best is what happens when you help someone. If you are buffing a teammate, they roll your die. Your bless, your d4, in their hands, on the floor, in front of the whole party. Helping somebody becomes a physical thing that happens in the room rather than a number that quietly appears in their total.

Damage dice are coming. And the camera moves with the moment — over the shoulder when it is yours, pulled back to tactical when it is the party’s, and all the way out to a tabletop view when you want to see the whole board. Dice will be customisable, because of course they will. Everybody has a set.

I am not a game designer. This is the decision I would defend hardest anyway.

What it unlocked Dice as objects instead of popups — and the one moment in a co-op game where helping a friend is something the table watches happen.
Summer 2026 · the encounter gets heavy

I kept wishing I had tools in here

The old encounter system started feeling like a real application. That sounds like a compliment and it is not one. Every new thing I wanted to add was more complex than the last, and I was spending my time working around the module instead of with it.

What I kept thinking, over and over, was: I wish I had tools inside this encounter. Something whose whole job was managing the clock. Something for intel — who knows what. Components that would take the orchestrating off my hands so the encounter could just be the encounter.

Then the turn, and it is my favourite realisation in the whole project:

The encounter was also one of those tools.

It was never the top of the stack. It was a peer. Pull the clock out, pull intel out, pull the record and the interruptions out, and what is left is not a diminished encounter — it is a composable one. And the thing that actually ties them all together is resolution.

From the record

What the old module had become, in my own words at the time: one type spread across twenty files owning combat, movement, fog of war, lock-picking, death saves and dungeon generation. “A tool does things its author never anticipated, because its pieces compose. What I had was exhaustive. You don’t build with it; you file an issue against it.”

What it unlocked The clock, intel, the record and interruptions became four small things I could reuse anywhere — and the encounter stopped being the place everything had to live.
August 2026 · the second time

If we need a new thing, we get a new machine

With resolution running the show, every verb in the game becomes its own little machine, and resolution is one driver that does not care which machine it is holding.

An attack is a machine: gather the attack roll, gather the damage, done. But a fireball does not roll to hit at all — so a fireball is just a machine that skips that step. Dragon breath, the same. Nobody special-cases anything; the machine simply has different steps.

And because every step boundary is plain data rather than a call stack, a machine can be paused. Interrupted. Sent back to the player for a confirmation. Written to disk, picked up again, and run to the end. That is what makes a reaction real: a monster answering automatically and a human being asked are the same step, answered by different things.

I thought I had it all.

And I think I probably did — for a single dungeon.

What it unlocked A new kind of thing became a new machine, not a new branch in the old one. Sixteen days after that decision, the old module was deleted.
Now · the third time, and the real one

But what would make the game good?

Here is the question that broke it open, and it is not an engineering question at all.

What would make a streamer want to buy this? What would make it compelling for their subscribers to actually play, week after week, rather than watch once? A perfectly composable dungeon crawler is still a dungeon crawler.

That is where the living world came from.

When somebody does something in this world, what comes out is not “success” or “failure”. It is a fact, with a list of who witnessed it. Nothing anywhere stores what the bandit camp believes — each observer works out its own version of the present by folding together only the things it actually saw.

Do that, and a whole category of things you would normally have to build stop needing to be built. Stealth is not a mechanic bolted on the side — it is authorship of your own audience. A disguise blown in front of exactly one guard is a fact with a very short witness list. The rogue can see a secret door the fighter genuinely cannot.

For a streamer, that is the good part: the camera sees everything, and each character sees only their own feed. Dramatic irony as a property of the engine rather than something the person running the game has to hold in their head.

And because a whole Discord server writes into one shared record, failure at scale starts generating content by itself. A rescue that goes badly across three different parties turns the hostages nobody saved into the antagonist roster for whatever comes next. Nobody authored that. The community’s own history did.

What it unlocked A reason for the world to matter between sessions — which is the difference between a dungeon somebody clears and a place a community lives in.

What I did not build

I should be straight about this, because the story reads better than the repository does.

On day one I wrote a mock-up of a Discord command showing a wandering merchant called Grimsby, in a town called Millhaven, with two days left before he moved on. There is no Grimsby. There is no weather, no autumn festival, no faction quietly shifting while you sleep. There is still no Artificer. An idea I wrote down at half past one in the morning — that character creation should not have to know what a Monk is — was never built, and the mess it was written to clean up is still sitting in that file.

The half of that day-one dream that did ship is the half I would not have predicted: the world remembering, per person, at the scale of a whole server.

I am not going to pretend the halfling is my work. Somebody at Synty modelled him. What I built is the reason he can pick up a club that is obviously too big for him and have the rules agree that he may try, and have the room remember that he did, and have the skeleton in the corner not know about it yet.

Five years out. One pull request that only tidied something up. A character sheet you could fill in inside a chat window, and a room drawn in text characters. Fourteen months building a thing nobody could look at, and four months away building the tools that let me build it faster.

Three times now I have thought I had everything I needed, and been wrong at a bigger scale each time. I have stopped expecting that feeling to be accurate. What I have instead is better, and it is the honest place to end:

I might not have everything I will need. But it currently looks like I have a place to put everything we want to build.

The club is roughly one halfling long. I am very happy with it.
